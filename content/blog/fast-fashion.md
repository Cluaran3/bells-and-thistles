title = 'Under Pressure: The Doorman Fallacy and the Fast Fashion Dilemma of Technical Writing in the Age of AI'

Technical writing as a domain is going through a perfect storm. You can feel the tides while browsing professional social media. And while both real and self-proclaimed experts on LinkedIn (part of them sporting the 'Open to work' green badge too many IT professionals are forced to wear in 2026) argue that AI actually makes technical writers more needed than ever rather than obsolete, a discordant chorus at r/technicalwriting laments real-life layoffs, vindictively prophesies massive rehiring as soon as the AI bubble bursts, or rather desperately seeks ways out of the profession.

My own glass is both half full and half empty. I can clearly see AI advent puts the screws to technical writers in at least three ways:
- The first and most obvious one: it promises to automate the role itself because, well, it can write. And while it might be clear for an insider that actually writing texts is only a fraction of a real job, it is definitely not so evident for a board member responsible for cost-cutting. If you write customer-facing documentation, you might be in a safer position because potential reputational damage is at stake, and it is something the board would rather avoid because it can eat into the company's bottom line. The importance of internal documentation, on the other hand, can be easily overlooked from above: if a developer spends several hours trying to find their way through neatly structured but overall shitty documentation — well, who cares, after all, they have so much more time now since AI does the code.
- Now you must run as fast as you can just to stay in place while the market is squeezing and demand for efficiency rises. However, there are no industry-wide standards on using AI tools in technical writing, nor can there be, given the pace of change. So the only way is to adapt and improvise yourself while closely monitoring the lifehacks and insights the community is ready to provide. Sometimes this can make you feel emboldened, sometimes confused and exhausted, but in both cases it consumes lots of time and effort besides your actual work.
- You scarcely have any time left for experiments and crafting your tools because developers empowered with AI can now produce new features in almost no time; features you have to describe but without any context and traditional artifacts the SDLC used to produce.

From this perspective, just another LinkedIn post on the future of technical writing and importance of human expertise may sound like wishful thinking. At the same time, I believe colleagues are doing a great job advocating the importance of the role itself to a general audience. Since what we witness here is a perfect [Doorman Fallacy](https://medium.com/@cristina.imre/the-doorman-fallacy-the-hidden-cost-of-blind-efficiency-e3f415fee689) case: the real value of good technical documentation is not always obvious even to end users. On the contrary, it is usually taken for granted until something goes wrong. And even in that case, it takes some experience and a well-trained eye before you can confidently jump to the conclusion that poor documentation is to blame for bad performance. That's why I don't really buy into expectations of [massive rehiring](https://www.forbes.com/sites/terdawn-deboe/2026/05/21/companies-fired-workers-for-ai-now-they-want-them-back/), at least in this domain and in this timeline.

Don't get me wrong, it would be a real pleasure to see companies quietly admitting their poor judgment — and some of them would certainly do so. However, what we will realistically see on a general scale is a rapid deterioration of documentation quality and people who get used to it, because humanity is just too ready to trade quality[^1] for ease and abundance. Just like fast fashion conquered the market pushing bespoke and designed-to-last clothing to premium and/or niche segments, the verbose, vague but polished-looking AI-generated documentation will inevitably take the lion's share everywhere except for critical spheres where human lives — or, in the boardroom's arithmetic, financial losses — are at stake. We can even see the 'human-written documentation' as a new marketing label defining a 'premium' product. And of course we shall not forget the branch of the multiverse where humans stop reading documentation altogether and agents try to build stuff relying on incomplete or misleading instructions produced by other agents.

But until then, I'll continue to run as fast as I can in hopes of securing myself a decent place in this AI Wild West.

[^1]: Let's be clear here: AI-generated documentation doesn't necessarily mean poor quality, just as human-written documentation can sometimes be crap too. Rather, any documentation created without supervision and stripped of complex (and, more often than not, implicit) context is a recipe for disaster.
